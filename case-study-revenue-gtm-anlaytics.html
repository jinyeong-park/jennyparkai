The Problem

Finance, RevOps, and CS Were Each Looking at a Different Number

Revenue Fragmentation: Finance tracked CRM bookings ($11.8M). RevOps tracked weighted pipeline ($9.8M). Customer Success monitored NRR on the renewal base. No single view connected them, and no one could answer the CFO's question: how much of our booked revenue will we actually collect?

Invisible Pipeline Risk: The CRM contained deals with no activity in 90+ days that were still counted in the forecast. $4.0M in pipeline carried two or more risk flags. Executives were using a raw pipeline number that overstated closeable revenue by over $23M.

The core problem was not a reporting gap. It was a structural one: four domains of revenue data (CRM, billing, pipeline, customer health) had never been joined into a single model. Every team was correct about their own slice. Nobody could see the whole picture.

Approach

dbt Staging → Mart → Streamlit Intelligence Layer

1. Three-Layer dbt Data Architecture

Raw tables (accounts, leads, opportunities, contracts, invoices, payments, usage, support) were standardised in dbt staging models with consistent types, field names, and null handling.

  • Staging models: Type-cast raw tables, rename fields to a consistent convention, handle nulls.
  • Intermediate models: Join across domains — match leads to accounts, score pipeline risk, compute lifecycle timelines, build customer health composites.
  • Mart models: Pre-aggregated facts for each page: fct_revenue_reconciliation, fct_gtm_funnel, fct_pipeline, fct_customer_health.

2. Shared Dashboard Component System

All five Streamlit pages share a single theme.py that defines visual primitives: hero(), kpi(), insight(), alert(), story_step(), and action_box(). Each page uses the same primitives with different data — making the visual language consistent and the code maintainable.

3. Deterministic Risk Scoring

Rather than a black-box propensity model, pipeline risk was scored using three explicit boolean flags that RevOps could immediately verify and act on.

  • is_past_due: Close date has passed without a stage change.
  • is_stale: No activity recorded in 30+ days.
  • has_repeated_slip: Close date has been moved at least twice.

Any deal with two or more flags becomes High Risk. Stage win rates (5% / 15% / 35% / 65%) were hard-coded as editable constants — the analytic pattern is correct; teams replace constants with historical actuals once they have enough closed-won history.

The largest single gap is payment risk ($1.7M), concentrated in customers with low health scores and overdue invoices beyond 60 days. The unbilled amount ($0.4M) points to contracts won but never invoiced — a billing workflow problem, not a sales problem. Surfacing these three buckets separately gave each team a clear, actionable number they could own.

Lead source quality analysis showed that Referral had the highest win rate — flagging underinvestment in the highest-converting channel — while Paid Search generated the most volume with a below-average close rate. The signal: optimise MQL qualification criteria before increasing spend.

The analysis also surfaced two data quality problems that distorted funnel metrics before any insight could be trusted: 14% of leads had no account_id (marketing spend invisible to pipeline reporting), and a set of orphan opportunities referenced accounts that no longer existed in Salesforce.
